# North American student representation

The U.S. edition's new version-3 intake uses an authored fictional cast. Preferred full names, skin tones, hair color/texture, self-described educational journeys, and everyday interests use separate cosmetic hash namespaces. None is a race or nationality classifier, and none changes the engine seed, ability, temperament, stress, research success, funding, or career odds.

The name pool has 96 whole preferred names in each of the male, female, and unspecified display pools. These are curated examples, not a census distribution. A surname establishes no demographic category. Names and introductions may be combined with every cosmetic tone; no name-to-skin or background-to-skin lookup exists. The palette extends from light to deep skin tones, and new portraits retain readable highlights instead of washing out darker colors.

Students introduce an educational journey and an everyday interest. The six journeys cover growing up and going to school locally, study in another country, an immigrant-family story, a move within the United States, community-college transfer, and time between school and college. Local upbringing and schooling receive half of the authored journey-selection weight; each of the other five paths receives one tenth. This makes a locally educated student an ordinary part of the cast. Moving within the United States, transferring from community college, or taking time between school and college can also be domestic paths. None of these introductions establishes citizenship, race, or nationality, and an immigrant-family story does not imply that the student was educated abroad. A separately selected first-generation college statement can accompany any journey. These are fictional story weights, not census proportions or estimates of real prevalence. Students are not uniformly given adversity narratives, financial difficulty, weak English, or other presumed deficits.

## Scope of institutional and immigration information

Background copy describes what a fictional student chooses to share. It does not assign citizenship, a visa, immigration status, work authorization, benefit eligibility, or a legal outcome. The game does not turn an “international study” introduction into an F-1 record.

Official USCIS guidance directs foreign students to their designated school official or responsible officer to establish applicable employment authorization and restrictions. This supports keeping those determinations outside a name/background generator. The referenced sheet is dated 2015 and is used only for that design boundary, not as current individualized immigration advice: [USCIS student employment information sheet](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/fact-sheets/FormI-9-FactSheet-for-Students.pdf).

Stanford's accounts describe first-generation experiences from both the United States and abroad, and its institutional reporting discusses differing definitions. The fictional module therefore uses a student's own college-attendance statement and does not equate first-generation status with immigrant status or low income: [Stanford first-generation family experiences](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/03/first-generation-students-family-weekend), [Stanford first-generation definitions and experiences](https://domannualreports.stanford.edu/making-the-meaning-of-first-generation-from-college-to-career/).

## Compatibility and agency

Korean intakes continue with version 2. Existing version-1 and version-2 names, PM/PM2 codes, drafts, and appearance generation remain unchanged. Only new English version-3 admissions use PM3 codes and the wider cosmetic palette. Old students do not gain background records; archived CVs contain no added demographic fields and retain their frozen images.

The first conversation begins by asking for the student's view: “What do you think, [name]?” or “[이름] 학생은 어떻게 생각해요?” The reply asks to develop a small question together. It conveys agency without inferring a personality or ability score.
